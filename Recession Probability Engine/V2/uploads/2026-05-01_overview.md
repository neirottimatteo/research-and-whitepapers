# Recession Probability Engine — Project Overview

*May 2026 — Matteo Neirotti*

---

## What this is

A machine learning system that produces a monthly probability estimate of a U.S. recession occurring within the next 12 months. The output is a single number between 0 and 100%. When that number crosses a calibrated threshold and stays there for at least three consecutive months, the model is considered to be signaling an alarm.

The goal was not to beat Wall Street. It was to build something rigorous enough to be useful: a system that catches every major recession on historical record with meaningful advance warning, while keeping false alarms rare enough that the signal retains credibility.

---

## The core problem

Predicting recessions is hard for several reasons that interact in nasty ways:

**Labels arrive late.** The NBER (the body that officially dates U.S. recessions) announces recession starts retroactively — often 6 to 12 months after the fact. This means that for any model trained on recent data, the ground truth is still being revised.

**Events bleed forward.** The model is asked to predict 12 months into the future. This means that a training sample from, say, early 2019 carries a label of "1" (recession incoming) simply because COVID hit in early 2020 — not because of any learnable economic pattern in 2019. If you don't handle this explicitly, the model learns spurious associations.

**False positives destroy credibility.** A model that cries wolf too often becomes useless. The real cost of a false alarm isn't just being wrong — it's that the next real alarm gets ignored.

**History is sparse.** There have been roughly 12 U.S. recessions since 1950. That's not a lot of positive examples to learn from.

Each of these problems required a deliberate design choice.

## Data

All data comes from FRED (Federal Reserve Economic Data), the St. Louis Fed's public macroeconomic database. About 60 series are fetched, cached locally, and transformed into monthly features.

The features span four thematic groups:

- **Labor market** — unemployment rate, Sahm rule indicator, jobless claims, payroll growth, part-time employment
- **Credit and financial conditions** — yield curve spreads (10Y–2Y, 10Y–3M), corporate bond spreads, high-yield OAS, VIX, bank lending standards
- **Real activity** — industrial production, housing starts and permits, manufacturing hours, consumer sentiment, retail sales
- **Money and prices** — CPI, PPI, oil prices, real M2, Fed balance sheet

The data goes back as far as 1950 for some series, and as recently as the month before model training for others. Where a series didn't exist yet (VIX before 1990, for example), the corresponding feature is left as a proper missing value — not imputed, not zeroed out.

## The multi-dataset design

Rather than feed all 55+ features into a single model and hope for the best, the features are organized into five dataset views:

| Dataset | Start | Features | Perspective |
|---------|-------|----------|-------------|
| A | 1950 | 4 | Classic cycle signals (unemployment, production) |
| B | 1977 | 8 | Modern macro (yield curve, housing, sentiment) |
| C | 1939 | 12+ | Long history including pre-WWII cycles |
| D | 1986 | 9 | Financial stress (credit spreads, VIX) |
| E | 1950 | 55+ | Full combination — the production dataset |

Datasets A through D were developed first, each capturing a different economic lens. Dataset E is a union of all of them, including several additional features that were tested and validated.

## The models

Two gradient boosting algorithms form the production ensemble:

**XGBoost** — the primary model. Accelerated by a GPU (CUDA). Handles missing values natively, which matters for Dataset E where many series start after 1950. Weight in the ensemble: 70%.

**HistGradientBoosting** — sklearn's implementation of a similar algorithm. Slower training but consistent calibration. Also handles missing values natively. Weight: 30%.

These two were selected after evaluating ten algorithms (including logistic regression, random forests, SVC, MLP, AdaBoost, and naive Bayes). The gradient boosting family consistently dominated on AUC across all datasets and time periods. The 70/30 blend was determined by walk-forward calibration: heavier weight on XGBoost produced the best threshold stability.

No neural networks. No fancy architectures. The signal in macroeconomic data is slow-moving and relatively smooth — decision trees capture it well, and they're interpretable via SHAP.

## Key design decisions

### 1. The 24-month training cutoff

Every model is trained only on data older than 24 months from today. The recent 24 months are held out entirely — not for testing, just excluded.

Why: NBER revises recession dates. The forward label (is a recession coming in the next 12 months?) is structurally uncertain for recent observations. A model trained on the latest 12–24 months would be learning from labels that are still being written.

This is a conservative choice that costs some recency but eliminates a category of silent data leakage.

### 2. COVID censoring

The period from February 2019 through December 2020 is excluded from training labels entirely.

February–December 2019 gets excluded because samples from that window carry a recession label of "1" only because COVID arrived in Q1 2020. There was no learnable economic deterioration visible in 2019 that preceded COVID. Training on those samples would teach the model a spurious pattern.

2020 itself is excluded because COVID was an exogenous shock — a pandemic, not a business cycle. The model should not learn to detect pandemics.

Any false alarms the model generates in 2020 are also excluded from the false-positive count in validation. This is fair: a model that was never trained on COVID can't be expected to stay quiet during it.

### 3. Walk-forward validation

Standard cross-validation is wrong for time series. If you train on fold 3 and test on fold 1, you're using future data to predict the past. Walk-forward validation fixes this.

Each validation fold trains on all data up to a given point in history, then tests on everything that follows. Folds expand forward in time, adding one historical recession at a time. This mirrors how the model would actually be deployed: you only ever know what happened before today.

The metrics reported per fold are: AUC (discrimination ability), number of recessions caught, average lead time (months of advance warning), and false-positive rate.

### 4. Threshold calibration

A model outputs a probability. To trigger an alarm, you need a threshold: "fire when probability exceeds X%." That threshold is calibrated separately from training, using the full labeled history.

The calibration objective is: find the threshold that catches the maximum number of historical recessions with the longest average lead time, while keeping dangerous false alarms (persistent above-threshold streaks that don't precede a recession) as rare as possible.

Calibrated thresholds vary by model. XGBoost alone on Dataset E calibrates to 80%. The 70/30 ensemble calibrates to 85%. These high values reflect the model's confidence — when it's sure, it's really sure.

### 5. The confirmed filter — the most important design choice

Raw probability output is noisy. The model might spike above threshold for a single month and then drop back. That one-month spike is not a useful signal.

The confirmed filter requires that the probability stay above threshold for at least three consecutive months before the alarm is considered active. Below-threshold or short-duration spikes are zeroed out.

**This is the difference between the signal being credible and the signal being terrifying.**

Without the confirmed filter, the model's raw output shows elevated probabilities during several periods that don't correspond to actual recessions — 2011 (European debt crisis), 2015–2016 (China slowdown scare), early 2023 (rate-hike anxiety). These are real economic stress episodes, and the model is correct to show elevated concern. But they didn't become recessions.

With the confirmed filter, those episodes are suppressed. The alarm fires only when the model stays elevated for a sustained period — which historically only happens when a real recession follows.

The confirmed filter does not affect AUC. It doesn't change what the model learned. It's a signal-processing step applied to the output, and it makes the difference between a tool that requires constant reinterpretation and one that gives clear, actionable signals.

## Feature validation — Monte Carlo ablation

Before finalizing the production feature set, every feature was subjected to a Monte Carlo ablation test:

- **Drop-mode:** Run 1000 iterations. In each iteration, randomly drop a subset of features and record whether model performance (AUC and false-positive rate) improves or worsens. A feature that consistently causes false positives when present gets flagged for removal.
- **Add-mode:** Tested 25 candidate new features (credit tightening surveys, loan delinquency rates, job openings, Fed balance sheet, yield spread variants). Each was inserted individually and the same Monte Carlo procedure was run.

Results:
- Six features initially flagged by drop-mode were temporarily removed. Walk-forward validation showed worse performance without them. They were restored. The ablation score captures marginal effects; feature interactions matter.
- All 25 new candidate features failed the add-mode test — every single one either hurt AUC, raised the false-positive rate, or both. None were added to the production baseline.

The 25 new features remain available in the codebase under a separate `--feature-set full` flag. The evidence argues against using them in production. The baseline feature set is unchanged.

## SHAP explainability

Every monthly prediction is accompanied by a SHAP decomposition — a breakdown of which features pushed the probability up or down, and by how much.

Three chart types are produced:
- **Summary:** Overall feature importance ranked by average absolute contribution across all months
- **Timeline:** Feature contributions as time series — shows which factors were driving the signal in each historical period
- **Waterfall:** Single-month breakdown — shows what the model saw in a specific month and how each feature contributed to that month's probability

This matters for two reasons. First, it keeps the model honest — if the alarm is firing because of a single obscure feature, that's visible. Second, it connects the output to economic intuition: you can ask "why does the model think a recession is likely now?" and get a real answer.

## What the model currently says

As of the most recent run (May 2026), the ensemble produces an elevated probability. Whether that probability crosses the confirmed threshold for three consecutive months determines whether the model considers itself in alarm mode.

The model is not a forecast in the journalistic sense. It does not predict GDP. It does not predict unemployment. It produces a probability that economic conditions over the next 12 months will include a period of broad contraction as subsequently recognized by the NBER.

That is a narrower, more precise claim — and one that can be evaluated historically with clear right/wrong answers.

---

## What this is not

This is not a trading system. It is not backtested in any way that accounts for transaction costs, slippage, or the fact that markets often price in recessions before they're confirmed.

It is not a real-time system. It runs monthly when triggered and uses data cached from FRED's API.

It does not forecast severity, duration, or geographic scope of a potential recession.

It is a signal — one well-calibrated, historically validated signal — designed to give sustained, early warning that economic conditions are deteriorating in a pattern consistent with recession precursors.
